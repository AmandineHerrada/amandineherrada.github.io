abstract: The impact of chemical contaminants such as toxic metals on wildlife is a growing concern for populations’ health and viability in the wild. In particular, long-term exposure to low doses of toxic metals may affect fitness-related traits such as survival. Yet, demographic consequences of chemical contaminants remain largely understudied in mammals. Using detailed longitudinal studies of more than 400 roe deer (Capreolus capreolus) monitored in two forest populations in France, we aimed to fill this knowledge gap by assessing the relationship between hair concentrations of multiple toxic metals and annual survival probabilities. First, we identified patterns of toxic metal exposure from the concentrations of nine toxic metals using multivariate analyses, reporting overall relatively low levels of exposure. Then, we applied multistate mark-recapture models to test whether annual survival probabilities were influenced by toxic metal exposure, and whether these effects varied with age, year, or sex. We found evidence that toxic metal exposure was associated with survival probabilities in both populations, differently according to age and population. In the Trois-Fontaines population, annual survival decreased with increasing concentrations of cadmium and mercury in juvenile and old individuals. In the Chizé population, annual survival was lowest in individuals with both the lowest and highest concentrations of these two metals, independently of age. We did not detect any effect of other toxic metals (i.e., aluminium, antimony, arsenic, lead, strontium, thallium, titanium) on roe deer annual survival in either population. These findings provide rare empirical evidence that even low-level exposure to toxic metals may affect a key fitness component such as survival in a long-lived mammal in the wild. They also underscore the need to further investigate the demographic consequences of contaminant exposure in natural populations, and to investigate the underlying physiological mechanisms.
